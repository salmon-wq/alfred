# AI News Brief — 2026-11-05

## Overview
AI's physical footprint met the ballot box this week: voters rejected data centers almost everywhere they were asked, while capital and coding-agent consolidation kept moving at full speed.

## Top Stories

### 1. Voters Reject Data Centers in Nearly Every Midterm Ballot Measure
**Source**: CNBC, Fortune, Forbes | **Category**: Policy

Voters turned down data centers in every local ballot measure except one in the November 3 midterms (unofficial results), CNBC reports. Ohio held 21 of roughly 41 measures nationwide, and at least 14 Ohio municipalities passed bans or restrictions. Trenton's ban passed 80–20, and Wilmington voters rejected rezoning by about 80% across four referendums. Local permitting is now a real bottleneck for AI compute buildout, separate from power and chip supply.

### 2. Anthropic's Amended S-1 Warns Recursive Self-Improvement Threshold May Be Near
**Source**: TechCrunch, Scientific American | **Category**: Policy / Safety

In an S-1/A filed with the SEC on November 2 ahead of its planned Nasdaq listing, Anthropic said it may soon reach its own threshold for recursive self-improvement, though it has "not designated any of our current models as exceeding this threshold." The filing says Claude wrote over 90% of contributions to Anthropic's codebase in September 2026, and it warns that safety measures "could slow our capability gains relative to competitors." Some coverage overstates the claim. The filing does not say Anthropic has built a self-improving AI, and skeptics such as Georgia Tech's Mark Riedl call the framing hype.

### 3. OpenAI to Acquire Pierre Computer, Taking Codex Closer to GitHub's Turf
**Source**: Investing.com, DevOps.com | **Category**: Industry

OpenAI announced on November 4 that it will acquire Pierre Computer Company, maker of the Git hosting service Code Storage and the open-source Trees, Diffs, and Highlights libraries. Some of those libraries already run inside Codex. Terms were not disclosed, and the deal has not closed. Following the Astral and Ona deals, OpenAI now owns more of the developer stack under Codex, which puts it in more direct competition with Microsoft-owned GitHub.

### 4. Fal Reportedly Raises at a $16 Billion Valuation
**Source**: Bloomberg | **Category**: Industry

AI inference startup Fal has raised new capital at a $16 billion valuation, Bloomberg reported on November 3. That is roughly double its last reported mark and in line with the $15–20 billion talks The Information described in September. Investors keep paying high prices for inference infrastructure, even as concern about AI-capex returns grows (see story 5).

### 5. Melius Cuts Nvidia Target to $315 on AI-Financing Risk
**Source**: Melius Research via market press | **Category**: Industry

Melius Research kept its Buy rating on Nvidia but cut its price target to $315 from $420. It cited financing risk in the AI buildout, not weak demand, and also cut its Marvell target to $300. The cut came during a broader selloff driven by doubts that hyperscaler AI spending will earn adequate returns. Nvidia reports quarterly results in mid-November.

### 6. Salesforce Signs Definitive Agreement to Acquire Cresta
**Source**: Salesforce Newsroom | **Category**: Industry

Salesforce agreed to buy Cresta, a voice-first AI company for contact centers. It plans to build Cresta's real-time conversation intelligence and agentic features into Service Cloud. The deal is expected to close in Q1 of fiscal 2028, pending regulatory approval, and terms were not disclosed. Enterprise software vendors are still buying their agent capabilities instead of building them.

### 7. Google Faces Pushback Over School AI Controls That Also Disable Search
**Source**: The New York Times (via aggregators; not independently confirmed) | **Category**: Products / Policy

Google is reportedly facing criticism because schools that turn off its AI tools also lose Search. A Google education VP told the Times the two are "deeply integrated." The criticism builds on Common Sense Media's July report, which rated Google Search "Unacceptable Risk" for children because AI Overviews can't be switched off separately. The dispute raises a broader question: can AI features that are bundled into core products be regulated separately from those products?
