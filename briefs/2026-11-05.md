# AI News Brief — 2026-11-05

## Overview
The midterm results landed hard on AI. Voters turned down data centers in all but one of nearly 40 local ballot measures. The industry's super PACs got little for their money. Asian chip stocks sold off on fears of tighter U.S. data-center rules. Arm and Qualcomm both beat estimates, and both fell after hours anyway.

## Top Stories

### 1. Voters Reject Data Centers in Every Local Ballot Measure but One
**Source**: CNBC / Fortune / Forbes / Capital B News | **Category**: Policy

Voters in 10 states considered nearly 40 data-center-related measures on Tuesday's ballot. In early results compiled by CNBC from Ballotpedia data, voters sided against data centers in every measure except one in Harwood, North Dakota. In Ohio, at least 14 municipalities passed bans or restrictions, and about 80% of Wilmington voters rejected four rezoning referendums tied to a single project. Nearly 80% of voters in Joliet, Illinois, backed restrictions. All counts are unofficial and may change before certification.

### 2. Asian Chip Stocks Slide on Fears of Tighter U.S. Data-Center Rules
**Source**: AP (via KRMG) / FinancialJuice / Kotak Neo | **Category**: Markets

Asian chipmakers sold off on Nov. 5 as investors priced in a less AI-friendly U.S. Congress. Intraday reports put the Kospi down between 3.25% and 5.3%, with Samsung Electronics down about 7.6% and SK Hynix down about 9.4%. Test-equipment maker Advantest fell about 15% in Tokyo. Chinese tech moved the other way: Tencent and Alibaba each rose more than 6%, lifting the Hang Seng Tech index about 5%. A widely shared figure of $550B in lost market value comes from a single crypto-news source and is unverified.

### 3. AI Industry Super PACs Get Little Return on Midterm Spending
**Source**: TIME / The Next Web | **Category**: Policy

Early analyses suggest AI-aligned super PACs spent about $79M across 68 races and lost their largest general-election bets. Leading the Future, the biggest network, spent about $10M in the general election, mostly on safe seats, and reportedly has just over $20M left. TIME reports that some major donors, including OpenAI's Greg Brockman, pulled back from promised contributions. TIME also cites a survey in which three-quarters of likely voters said AI super PAC money would make them less likely to support a candidate.

### 4. Arm Posts Record Revenue, but Royalties Miss and Shares Fall
**Source**: Reuters (via MarketScreener) / Alpha Spread | **Category**: Industry

Arm's fiscal Q2 revenue rose 27% year over year to a record of about $1.44B, ahead of the roughly $1.38B consensus. Adjusted EPS was $0.52, against an expected $0.47. Data-center royalties more than doubled. Total royalty revenue, however, rose only 8% to $670M, below the roughly $721M analysts expected, and Q3 guidance of about $1.54B was barely above consensus. Reported after-hours declines ranged from 2% to 5%.

### 5. Qualcomm Beats on Revenue but Guidance Disappoints
**Source**: Qualcomm earnings release / The Elec | **Category**: Industry

Qualcomm reported fiscal Q4 revenue of about $10.4B and non-GAAP EPS of $2.17, at the high end of its guidance. Handset revenue fell 26% year over year to $5.14B. Qualcomm expects more than $650M of data-center revenue in the December quarter as custom-silicon shipments begin, and it reiterated a fiscal 2027 data-center revenue target of $5B. The Elec reports shares fell more than 8% after hours because the guidance midpoint of $2.25 EPS came in just below the $2.27 consensus.

## Key Themes
- **Local politics now constrains AI infrastructure**: the ballot-measure results are the clearest evidence yet that data-center siting has become a voter-level fight. Markets reacted within a day.
- **AI money has not bought political influence**: by these early accounts, roughly $79M in super PAC spending did little, and being linked to AI money now looks like a liability for candidates.

- **Investors are separating AI exposure by region**: Korean and Japanese chip stocks fell while Chinese tech rallied, showing investors treat U.S. regulatory risk as specific to certain countries.
