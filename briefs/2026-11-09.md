### 1. Nvidia's board opens an independent probe into Jensen Huang
**Source**: Bloomberg, CNBC, NPR, CBS, NBC | **Category**: Industry

On Sunday, Nov 8, Nvidia's board opened an investigation led by a committee of independent directors with outside counsel. It followed a New York Times report that U.S. intelligence officials believe Alice Huang, an executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed, was passing information to Beijing and had a "close relationship" with the CEO. In a staff email, Huang admitted to an affair and wrote "there is no excuse for what I did." He remains CEO, and the board says it has confidence in him. Shares fell roughly 5–6% in Monday premarket trading (CNBC and Bloomberg give different figures). Republican chairs of the House China, foreign affairs and intelligence committees have asked him for a briefing. China's Foreign Ministry called the spy narrative "unsubstantiated." The governance question now sits on the AI trade's most important stock, in a week when Nvidia was already under pressure on financing risk.

### 3. Five UK banks block AI agents from customer accounts
**Source**: The Telegraph (via Dealroom, FourWeekMBA), American Banker, Hunton | **Category**: Policy

The Telegraph reported on Nov 8 that HSBC, Barclays, Lloyds, NatWest and Nationwide are blocking or restricting third-party AI agents that can transact on a customer's behalf. The UK moves look precautionary: Lloyds says it has seen no agent activity on its accounts. They follow US moves in mid-October by JPMorgan, Citi, Wells Fargo, Schwab and others. Some of those banks reported agents logging in with customer credentials, and Wells Fargo rewrote its terms to put agent risk on the customer. The FCA is working with the industry on agent rules but leaves access decisions to the banks. Agentic commerce is meeting its first institutional wall, and the unresolved issue is liability for an agent that goes beyond its instructions.

### 4. Claude Code's "don't ask again" leaves secrets in public repos
**Source**: Apiiro (via The Hacker News, Cybernews, GBHackers) | **Category**: Security

Apiiro reports that choosing "Yes, and don't ask again" saves the full approved command, including any inline password or token, to `.claude/settings.local.json`. Apiiro scanned 104,185 public copies of that file and found 1,122 containing 1,379 distinct secrets: 379 database passwords, 43 SSH logins, 42 GitHub/GitLab/npm tokens and 23 AWS keys. Other coverage cites 2,539 secrets across 1,994 repositories, and the two counts have not been reconciled. Anthropic's Boris Cherny noted that Claude Code gitignores the file when it creates it, so the exposed copies were likely committed by hand or through repo-wide add rules. Only the git config ignores it, not the project's `.gitignore`, so that protection does not travel with a clone. The advice is to rotate first, then untrack the file and add an explicit ignore rule. *(This repository does not track the file on any branch, but `.gitignore` does not list it either.)*

### 5. Tencent ships Hy4, trained without offensive cyber data
**Source**: Reuters (via The Star, KFGO), Tencent, Technology.org | **Category**: Models

Tencent released Hy4 on Nov 4. It says it filtered exploit development, reverse engineering and penetration-testing material out of training and kept defensive work such as vulnerability remediation. Its Xuanwu and Keen security labs report "no significant concerns" in the offensive areas they tested. No cyber benchmark score was published, the evaluation is Tencent's own, and open weights were promised "in the coming days." A capability-restricted open model is a notable answer to the rising worry about cyber-capable releases.
