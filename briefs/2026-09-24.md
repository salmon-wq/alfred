### 1. Altman and Amodei ask the UN Security Council for international AI standards
**Source**: CNN Business / Bloomberg / CoinTelegraph | **Category**: Policy

French Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot chaired the 23 September briefing. Sam Altman called for international standards for "measuring capabilities, assessing risks, determining whether safeguards are sufficient and preserving meaningful human oversight." He also said major decisions should be made by democratic institutions "accountable to the people they serve." That wording could exclude China. Dario Amodei, speaking by video, warned that "AI could be a risk to humanity as a whole" and offered three proposals: (1) narrow global agreements, starting with a ban on using AI to build biological weapons; (2) evaluation and verification systems so countries can check each other's commitments; (3) common testing standards plus a notification system for AI security incidents. Clément Delangue and Yoshua Bengio also briefed. Reuters reported that DeepSeek and Moonshot were invited to make statements. I could not confirm who, if anyone, spoke for them. ### 3. Cisco Talos discloses CLOSEDQUORUM, the first reported autonomous AI command-and-control implant
**Source**: Talos Intelligence / SC Media / Tech Times | **Category**: Security

Talos published its analysis on 22 September. The Windows implant consults four commercial LLMs (DeepSeek, Qwen, Mistral and Google Gemini), and has them vote on which attack action to take next. It then runs the winner. After deployment it needs no human operator. The recovered binary contained placeholder API keys and a sample Discord webhook, so Talos judges that it had not yet been used in live campaigns. Talos also open-sourced CAIRN, a metadata-first toolkit for hunting malware that has AI built in. For defenders, traffic from endpoints to model-provider APIs is now worth watching as an indicator of compromise.

### 4. Anthropic's new biology lab reports Claude found an unknown CRISPR-like phage system
**Source**: Anthropic / TechCrunch / Interesting Engineering | **Category**: Research

Anthropic launched a life-sciences research group and announced its first result. About 950 agents ran for 21 hours and used 210M tokens. They screened 200,000 reverse transcriptases, narrowed 3,500 candidates to 20 systems, and flagged one: array-associated reverse transcriptases (ART). ART is found mainly in bacteriophages and has three parts: the RT, a partner gene next to it, and a long array of evenly spaced DNA repeats. What it does is not yet known, and nobody has shown it can be used for gene editing. Feng Zhang (MIT/Broad) reviewed the preprint and called it "intriguing and worthy of further investigation." For now it is a finding that needs follow-up, not a new tool.

### 6. First independent numbers on GPT-6 Sol and Luna: cheaper and more cautious, not smarter
**Source**: Artificial Analysis / ComputingForGeeks / Vellum | **Category**: Models

Artificial Analysis measured the hallucination rate falling from 92% to 60% for Sol (max) and from 93% to 77% for Luna (max). Most of Sol's gain comes from declining to answer: it attempts 83% of questions, against 99% for GPT-5.6 Sol, so accuracy falls 5 points, from 59% to 54%. The gains elsewhere are small: AutomationBench-AA (Sol 62% vs 60%, Luna 53% vs 50%) and Terminal-Bench 4.0 (Sol 44% vs 40%, Luna 13% vs 12%). There are regressions on two knowledge-work evaluations. In a same-prompt DevOps comparison, ComputingForGeeks found Sol cost about a third of what Opus 5.5 cost on identical tasks. In short, the new models mostly improve cost efficiency, not capability.
